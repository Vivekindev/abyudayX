## Payment flows

### Card payment parties — cardholder, merchant, acquirer/PSP, card network, issuer
The **cardholder** pays and the **merchant** sells. The **acquirer/PSP** (Razorpay, Stripe, a bank) processes the payment for the merchant. The **card network** (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay) routes the authorisation by BIN and runs clearing and settlement. The **issuer** (the cardholder's bank) approves or declines and debits the account. Visa itself is the network: it doesn't hold accounts or lend money.
**In the interview:** naming all five correctly sets the tone for a payments design. [Full lesson →](#/item/payments-fundamentals)

### Authorization vs capture vs settlement — hold now, claim later, move money in batch
**Authorization**: a real-time issuer approval that places a **hold** on the funds. **Capture**: the merchant claims the held amount, immediately or later (hotels, e-commerce on shipment; partial capture is possible). **Clearing & settlement**: batch files exchanged through the network, with money moving between banks at T+1/T+2 and then paid out to the merchant. Uncaptured auths expire.
**In the interview:** explains why "payment succeeded" and "merchant got paid" are different events.

### UPI flow — payer PSP → NPCI → remitter bank → beneficiary bank
The payer's app (PhonePe, GPay) sends the request, with the UPI PIN, via its PSP bank to **NPCI's UPI switch**, which asks the **remitter bank** to debit, then the **beneficiary bank** to credit, and returns success. It's real-time and account-to-account, at huge volume. Timeouts create "deemed" or pending states, resolved through status checks and auto-reversal within RBI timelines.
**In the interview:** relevant for any India-focused payment design (PhonePe, Paytm, Razorpay).

### Payment state machine — CREATED → PROCESSING → AUTHORIZED/FAILED/UNKNOWN → CAPTURED → SETTLED/REFUNDED
Model payments as explicit states with allowed transitions only, each done as a **conditional update** (`WHERE status='PROCESSING'`) so it can't happen twice, with every transition recorded in an events/audit table. **UNKNOWN** is a first-class state for timeouts. Events published via an outbox drive the ledger, webhooks and notifications.
**In the interview:** draw the state machine. Interviewers love it. [Payment system case →](#/item/payment-system)

### Refunds, reversals & chargebacks — undoing money movement safely
A **void/reversal** cancels an authorisation before capture (no money moved). A **refund** returns captured money as a new, idempotent transaction (full or partial, never exceeding the captured amount). A **chargeback** is initiated by the cardholder through the issuer, so money is pulled from the merchant and the dispute workflow needs evidence. Each one posts its own ledger entries.
**In the interview:** "refunds are new transactions with their own idempotency keys and ledger postings."

### PSP webhooks & status polling — resolving UNKNOWN payments
Banks and PSPs report final status asynchronously. Receive their **webhooks** (verify the HMAC signature, dedupe by event ID, respond fast, process asynchronously), **poll** the status API with backoff for payments stuck in PROCESSING, and treat the daily **settlement file** as the final source of truth. Never mark a timeout as failed without confirmation.
**In the interview:** "timeout → UNKNOWN → webhook, poll or reconciliation decides."

## Money correctness

### Representing money — integer minor units or BigDecimal, never float
Binary floating point can't represent 0.1 exactly, so rounding errors accumulate. Store **integers in minor units** (paise: ₹499.00 = 49900) or a fixed-precision `DECIMAL`/`BigDecimal`, always with a currency code. Define rounding rules explicitly (banker's rounding, distributing leftover paise in splits). A `Money` value object in code prevents mistakes.
**In the interview:** say it unprompted in every money design.

### Idempotency at every hop — client→API, API→PSP, consumers, webhooks
Duplicates can arise at every boundary. **Client → API**: an Idempotency-Key header with a unique constraint and a stored response. **API → PSP/bank**: send your payment ID as their idempotency or reference key. **Kafka consumers**: an inbox table keyed by event ID. **Webhooks**: dedupe by event ID. Together, these make retries harmless.
**In the interview:** walk through each hop. That's what makes the answer "SDE-2 strong".

### Double-entry ledger — every transaction has balanced debits and credits
Every money movement is a transaction with ≥2 entries whose debits equal credits (customer wallet −₹1,000; merchant +₹980; fee revenue +₹20). Money can't appear or vanish from a bug, and the books always balance, which is checkable continuously. Balances = the sum of entries, cached in an account row updated in the same transaction.
**In the interview:** the foundation of wallets, banks and marketplaces. [Ledger lesson →](#/item/ledger-reconciliation)

### Ledger immutability — append-only entries, fix with reversing entries
Ledger entries are never UPDATEd or DELETEd (enforce with DB permissions or triggers). Mistakes are corrected with **reversing entries** followed by correct ones, so the full history stays auditable. Optionally hash-chain entries for tamper evidence. Old entries are archived but remain queryable for regulatory retention (7–10 years).
**In the interview:** "append-only, corrections by reversal" is the expected phrase.

### Reconciliation — match internal records with bank/PSP settlement files
A daily (and near-real-time for high-value flows) job matches internal payments and ledger entries against PSP and bank settlement reports by reference, amount and date. Mismatches go to an exceptions queue: auto-resolve known patterns (late success, fees, timing) and send the rest to ops. Three-way recon covers internal ledger ↔ PSP ↔ bank statement.
**In the interview:** every serious payment system has one. Include it in the design.

### Exactly-once money movement — at-least-once + idempotency + state guards + reconciliation
There's no magic exactly-once over unreliable networks. Combine **at-least-once** delivery and retries, **idempotent** operations at every hop, **state-machine guards** (conditional transitions), the **transactional outbox** for events, and **reconciliation** as the final safety net that detects and corrects any drift.
**In the interview:** say exactly this sentence when asked "how do you guarantee exactly once?"

### Hot accounts — sub-accounts and batching for high-write ledger accounts
Some accounts receive enormous write rates (the platform fee account, a big merchant's settlement account), and row locks on a single balance row become a bottleneck. Fixes: split into **N sub-accounts** (fee_0…fee_15, written randomly, summed for reporting), **batch** postings for internal accounts asynchronously, or keep a running balance computed from entries rather than locking per transaction.
**In the interview:** an advanced follow-up for ledger designs at scale.

## Compliance & risk

### PCI-DSS & tokenization — keep card numbers out of most systems
PCI-DSS is a security standard for systems that store, process or transmit card data, with heavy audit requirements. Minimise scope by **tokenising** card numbers at the edge: a hardened vault (an HSM-backed, segregated network) holds the PAN, and every other service sees only tokens. Card networks also issue **network tokens** for wallets and merchants. Never log PANs or CVVs (CVV must never be stored).
**In the interview:** "only the vault is in PCI scope; everything else uses tokens."

### Fraud & risk checks — rules, velocity limits, ML scoring in the payment path
Before authorising: **rules** (blocked BINs or countries, amount limits), **velocity checks** (number of transactions per card, device or IP per minute, using Redis counters or stream-processing state), and **ML risk scores** from features (device fingerprint, history, merchant risk), all within a strict latency budget (tens of milliseconds). Outcomes: approve, decline, or step-up authentication (3-DS/OTP). Feedback (chargebacks) retrains the models.
**In the interview:** "a synchronous risk check with a 50 ms budget, plus async deeper analysis."

### RBI data localisation & audit trails — data stays in India, immutable logs
RBI requires end-to-end payment data for Indian transactions to be stored only in India (multi-region DR therefore means Mumbai + Hyderabad, not Singapore). Regulated systems need immutable audit trails (who did what, when), retention for years, access controls and periodic audits. India's DPDP Act adds personal-data obligations.
**In the interview:** mentioning data residency in India-focused designs is a strong signal.

## Trading systems

### Order book & matching engine — price-time priority, single-threaded per symbol
Each symbol has bids (highest first) and asks (lowest first), stored as sorted price levels with FIFO queues per level, plus an order-ID map for O(1) cancels. Incoming orders match against the opposite side at the best prices (price-time priority). The engine runs **single-threaded per symbol or shard**: no locks, deterministic, replayable from a sequenced input journal.
**In the interview:** see the [stock exchange case](#/item/stock-exchange).

### Low-latency techniques — LMAX Disruptor, GC avoidance, kernel bypass
Trading paths care about microseconds and predictable tails: single-threaded business logic, lock-free ring buffers (**LMAX Disruptor**) between stages, allocation-free hot paths (object pools, primitives, off-heap memory) with ZGC, CPU pinning and busy-spinning, binary encodings (SBE), and **kernel-bypass** networking (DPDK/Onload) with colocation.
**In the interview:** naming a few of these impresses at Morgan Stanley / JPM. [Lesson →](#/item/low-latency-trading)

### Market data fan-out — multicast, conflation, sequence numbers
Exchanges publish order-book updates and trades to many subscribers via **UDP multicast**, with sequence numbers (to detect gaps) and snapshot/recovery channels. Brokers fan out to millions of app users over WebSockets, **conflating** updates (sending the latest price every 100–250 ms) because mobile clients can't consume every tick. Slow consumers must never block fast ones.
**In the interview:** relevant for "design Zerodha / Robinhood live prices".
